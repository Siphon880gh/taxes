
1099-NEC, Schedule C, Schedule E, Passive or Active Rental Income, Withheld Income vs Self Employee Tax

You may receive tax statements that are W2 or 1099-NEC from people or companies you work with. Or you may receive neither because they aren't obligated to or their business or your relationship is not structured for it.

You may receive 1099-NEC from companies you work with who don't classify you as an employee (Otherwise you receive W2 tax statements). Those incomes go into Schedule C so you can calculate the Profits and Losses. Since you are mostly in control as a freelancer or contractor or working as a business helping other companies, you likely have to upfront many costs. Whereas if you were an employee whose manner of work, time, and place, by legal definition, is controlled, the employer likely have purchased capital and all sorts of investments to make your job possible. Recall that NEC means non-employee compensation.

Due to the nature of being a freelancer/contractor/business, working with other companies or clients, they might not issue you a 1099-NEC tax statement either because they don't have to or they can't (due to the nature of the work being unstructured). Those custom incomes ALSO gets recorded into Schedule C.

Now if you had done employee work, you received a W2. Those DO NOT go into Schedule C.

If you have rental income, does it require day-to-day handling? Renting out to one family wouldn't (as a small landlord). And hiring a managing company to manage your apartment unit tenants wouldn't (as an investor or hands-off owner). All these questions classify the rental income as either passive income or active income. ALL PASSIVE INCOMES go into Schedule E. If you're a big firm that manages the tenants, you're likely to be working there day-to-day, then it's active income that rolls into Schedule C.

Rental income that are in Schedule E are passive and are considered likely smaller supplemental income (Hence Schedule E is called Supplemental Income). Schedule E rental income DOES NOT allow deductions as a Qualified Business Income (Form 8995) and marking yourself as Safe Harbor for Rental Income QBI Deduction (by creating a statement and signing that you've worked 250 hours and have a log of activities) would be fraudulent.

Lastly, another important consideration is withholding income. W2 employees because of the W4 certificate that you sign at hiring and returned to them, have become authorized to withhold your income, which they pay quarterly to IRS. If you're 1099-NEC or reporting custom income that don't have 1099-NEC, there's no withholding, and then you will act as both Employer and Employee, paying double the Federal Taxes, and the Social Security, and the Medicare! This totals to about 15.3% and is dubbed the Self-Employee Tax (Schedule SE), and you will likely be penalized with an "Underpayment Penalty". To pay quarterly, you pay at IRS.gov ahead of the $0 balance (ID.me authorization requires you to show your face on webcam so a human can check your face against your ID card on their file).

**If high earner:** There's a maximum amount of self-employee income that gets taxed and that's prefilled on Schedule SE line 7 (Usually 160-180k in the early 2020s). Paying quarterly estimates based on income **over** the cap ahead of the $0 balance at IRS.gov:
- ✅ The **Social Security portion (12.4%) stops** at the cap.
- The **Medicare portion (2.9%) continues** on all income. There is no cap on Medicare.
- For a total of 15.3% that totals both employer and employee portion

Self Employment tax is not related to the federal income tax. The federal income tax is based on **total taxable income**, and it scales up with higher income levels using marginal tax brackets. There’s **no upper limit** — the more you earn, the more you may owe.
