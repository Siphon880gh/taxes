
If you have more than $3,000 in capital losses* in a year, you can only deduct up to $3,000 on your tax return (or $1,500 if married filing separately). The leftover losses can be carried forward to future years — this is called a capital loss carryforward.

That's why it's important to give your tax preparer your previous year’s tax return — so they know how much loss is still available to deduct.
How it works:
Capital losses are used first to offset capital gains.
If there's still a loss left, you can deduct up to $3,000 of it from regular income (like wages or business income).
Anything beyond that gets carried forward to use in future tax years — until it's used up.
IRS Forms Where It’s Reported:
Schedule D (Form 1040) – Capital Gains and Losses
Look at Part III: Summary for total gains/losses.
Capital Loss Carryforward Worksheet
Found in the Schedule D instructions; helps calculate how much you can carry forward.
Form 1040, Line 7
This is where your net capital gain or loss from Schedule D goes.
In short:
The tax loss carryforward rule helps you reduce taxes in future years by letting you use past investment losses to offset future gains or income — as long as you follow IRS rules.
